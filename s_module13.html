Descriptive statistics

IAN 601 · Module 13

Descriptive statistics summarize the basic shape of a numeric variable.

  • Mean is the arithmetic average.
  • Median is the middle value.
  • Standard deviation measures spread.
  • Quartiles divide the data into four parts.
Do not stop at one number. Mean, median, and spread answer different questions.

Extreme values can pull the mean

Mean versus median

IAN 601 · Module 13

The mean uses every value, so an unusually large or small observation can move it. The median depends on the middle position and is usually more resistant to extremes.

MeanUseful when values are fairly balanced.
MedianOften useful for skewed data or strong outliers.
Original20, 25, 30, 35, 40Mean = 30 · Median = 30
Add one extreme value20, 25, 30, 35, 400Mean = 102 · Median = 30
Interpretation: the median can sometimes represent a typical observation better than the mean when the distribution is strongly skewed.

Visualize a distribution with a histogram

IAN 601 · Module 13

A histogram groups numeric values into bins and shows how many observations fall into each range.

  • Look for symmetry or skew.
  • Look for gaps or clusters.
  • Look for unusually distant values.
  • Remember that changing the number of bins can change the appearance.
A histogram answers: how are the numeric values distributed?

Boxplots summarize distributions

IAN 601 · Module 13

A boxplot compresses a distribution into a small visual summary using the median, quartiles, and whiskers.

  • The line inside the box is the median.
  • The box covers the interquartile range.
  • Whiskers show the typical range beyond the box.
  • Points beyond the whiskers may be flagged as outliers.
Boxplots are especially useful for comparing the same numeric variable across groups.

Bar charts for categorical comparisons

IAN 601 · Module 13

A bar chart is a good choice for category counts or category-level summary values.

  • Use bars for discrete categories.
  • Start the quantitative axis at zero when bar length represents magnitude.
  • Keep labels readable.
  • Avoid unnecessary 3D effects.
Do not confuse a bar chart with a histogram. Histograms display numeric distributions; bar charts display categories.

Scatter plots for numeric relationships

IAN 601 · Module 13

A scatter plot is useful when both variables are numeric and you want to inspect how they move together.

  • Direction: positive, negative, or no clear direction
  • Form: linear or curved
  • Strength: tight or widely scattered
  • Outliers: unusual points
Visual inspection comes before correlation. A single correlation value can hide nonlinear patterns or influential points.

Correlation can be misleading

IAN 601 · Module 13

A correlation coefficient describes linear association. It does not tell the entire story.

  • A curved relationship may have a low linear correlation.
  • One extreme point can strongly change the coefficient.
  • Subgroups can create or hide a pattern.
  • Restricted ranges can weaken an apparent relationship.
Better workflow1. Plot the variables2. Inspect outliers and groups3. Calculate correlation4. Interpret in context
Use correlation as one piece of evidence, not as a complete explanation.

estimate ± margin of errorsample mean → plausible range for the population mean under the method's assumptions

Titles, labels, scales, and clutter matter

Make charts easier to read

IAN 601 · Module 13

A chart can be technically correct and still communicate poorly.

  • Use a title that tells readers what the chart represents.
  • Label axes with meaningful variable names and units.
  • Use readable category labels.
  • Remove decoration that does not help interpretation.
  • Use consistent scales when comparing charts.
Avoid3D bars that distort lengthtruncated axes that exaggerate differencestoo many categories in one figureunexplained colors or abbreviations
Visualization is part of analysis. Design choices can change what a reader notices and how large a difference appears.

Line charts for time trends

IAN 601 · Module 13

A line chart connects measurements across ordered time and makes trend direction easier to see.

  • Sort the data by date first.
  • Use regular time intervals when possible.
  • Do not connect unrelated categories with a line.
Typical uses: daily visits, monthly sales, weekly website traffic, or yearly enrollment.

Describe evidence without overclaiming

Interpret carefully

IAN 601 · Module 13

Good wording

“West has the highest mean order amount in this sample.”

Too strong

“West customers always spend more.”

Good wording

“The sample shows a positive association between these variables.”

Too strong

“One variable causes the other.”

Keep the claim at the same level as the evidence. Descriptive statistics describe the sample. Causal conclusions require much stronger study designs and assumptions.

Describe

Use mean, median, standard deviation, quartiles, and counts to summarize the sample.
